vivekvar/turboquant: why it slows down on long contexts
=========================================================

dequant_keys = self._dequantize(self._quantized_keys)   # the WHOLE compressed blob, every step
keys_to_return = torch.cat([dequant_keys, self.keys, key_states], dim=-2)

There's no incremental/cached dequantization. So per-step cost grows with
total sequence length, meaning total generation cost grows faster than
linearly as the sequence gets longer — on top of attention's own cost. At
~4600 tokens that overhead is real and substantial; at the short prompt's
~300 tokens it's negligible.

This is exactly the structural gap mlx-vlm's TurboQuant avoids: its fused
Metal kernels compute attention directly on the packed representation
without ever materializing a full dequantized tensor.

This isn't an MPS-specific problem — it would show up on CUDA too at long
enough context, just later, since CUDA has more raw throughput to absorb
it before it dominates.

Simple analogy
---------------
It's like re-reading and rewriting your entire notebook from page 1 every
time someone adds a new sentence to a conversation, instead of just adding
the new sentence and leaving the old pages alone. Fine for a short
notebook, ruinous for a long one.


Why decompress at all? What problem is it solving?
====================================================

Two separate questions get tangled together here:

1. Why does it need to decompress at all -- why can't attention just work
   on the compressed bits directly?

   What's stored isn't really "a compressed vector" -- it's a small integer
   (say, 4 bits = a number 0-15) that says "this vector's shape is closest
   to entry #7 in a fixed lookup table of 16 possible directions." That's
   it. It's a pointer, not a number you can do math with.

   Attention math (Q . K, then softmax, then weighted sum of V) needs real
   floating-point numbers to multiply and add. You cannot dot-product a
   query against the integer 7 -- 7 isn't a vector, it's an index. Before
   attention can run, something has to say "index 7 means
   [0.12, -0.05, 0.88, ...]" and substitute the real numbers back in. That
   substitution step IS dequantization. It's not optional busywork -- it's
   the unavoidable translation from "compact code" back to "thing you can
   actually compute with."

   So the decompression itself isn't the design flaw. Some translation step
   has to happen somewhere. The flaw is how, and how often, this library
   does it.

2. Why redo it for the ENTIRE history, every single step, instead of
   smarter alternatives?

   This is the actual bug. There are two better ways to solve problem #1
   that this library doesn't use:

   - Cache the translation. Once you've decompressed token #50, you don't
     need to decompress it again for token #51 -- nothing about token #50
     changed. This library throws that away and redoes it from scratch
     every time.

   - Skip the translation entirely, cleverly. Since there are only 16
     possible lookup-table entries, you can precompute "how much does my
     CURRENT query agree with each of the 16 possible directions" once --
     16 numbers -- and then for every stored token, just pick the right one
     of those 16 precomputed numbers off a shelf instead of reconstructing
     a full vector and redoing the multiplication. This is the classic
     trick behind fast vector search (FAISS and friends), and it's what
     mlx-vlm's Metal kernels do -- they compute attention scores directly
     from the packed index using a small precomputed table, never
     materializing the full-size decompressed array at all.

   So the real answer to "what is decompressing solving": it's solving the
   fact that ordinary attention code only knows how to read real numbers,
   not compressed codes. That problem is real and has to be solved somehow.
   This particular library solves it the laziest possible way -- brute-force
   expand everything, every time -- instead of caching the expansion or
   using the lookup-table shortcut, which is why it gets slower as the
   conversation gets longer instead of staying cheap.
